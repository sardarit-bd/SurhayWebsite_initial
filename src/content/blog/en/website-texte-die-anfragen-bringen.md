Most company websites do not have a design problem. They have an ordering problem. The copy says in paragraph one how long the company has existed, in paragraph two that quality comes first, and in paragraph three something about passion. What the company solves, and for whom, appears — if at all — under the third subheading.

This article describes the order that works instead. It is not about phrasing but about sequence: changing the order gains you more than polishing adjectives ever will.

## The root error: writing from your own point of view

A visitor arrives with a question, not with an interest in your company history. In the first few seconds they decide three things: am I in the right place? Do these people understand my problem? What am I supposed to do now?

Copy that opens with "Since 1998 we have stood for …" answers none of those three. It answers a fourth that nobody asked.

The test is simple: mentally delete your company name from your homepage copy and insert a competitor's. Does the text remain true? Then it says nothing.

## The order that holds

**1. What you do for whom — in one sentence.** Not the vision, not the values. "We build websites for trade businesses that want to be found on Google." Specific enough that some readers will not feel addressed. That is exactly the purpose: a sentence that addresses everyone addresses nobody.

**2. The problem you solve — in their words.** Not "holistic digital transformation strategy" but "your website is ten years old and barely usable on a phone". People recognise their problem in the phrasing they would use themselves.

**3. What you do differently — with a verifiable commitment.** "Personal service" is not a commitment, because nobody claims the opposite. "A reply to every enquiry within 24 hours" is one — it can be broken, and that is precisely what makes it credible.

**4. The evidence.** A reference, a number, a quote. And if none of it exists: invent nothing. An honest "we are starting out, which is why you will find fixed prices here instead of references" reads stronger than a fabricated testimonial — and invented customer quotes are misleading advertising in most European jurisdictions.

**5. What to do now — a single path.** Not call and form and newsletter and download. One path, clearly named.

## Subheadings are the real copy

Almost nobody reads a website top to bottom. The usual pattern is: scroll, snag on a heading, enter there.

From which follows a hard rule: **your subheadings must tell the story on their own.** If someone reads only those, they must have understood what this is about and what to do.

The difference in practice:

- Weak: "Our services" — "About us" — "Contact"
- Load-bearing: "Three things, done right" — "Who actually builds your website" — "Let's talk about your project"

The first row is labelling. The second is an argument.

## The first-sentence test

Take every paragraph on your website and delete the first sentence. In most cases the paragraph improves. The first sentence is almost always a run-up: an introduction explaining that something is about to be explained.

Example:

> In today's digital world a professional website is more important than ever. We build websites tailored to your audience.

The first sentence is filler that appears on every second agency site. The second carries the meaning. Delete the first.

## Concrete numbers beat every adjective

Compare:

> We work quickly and reliably.

with:

> Your website goes live in five to seven weeks. We answer enquiries within one working day.

The second version is not prettier. It is verifiable — which is why it is believed. Wherever you can name a number, name it: duration, price, quantity, deadline. Where you have none, write a concrete sentence instead of a vague adjective.

## What belongs on which page

Now that websites have several pages again, the question of distribution arises. A rule of thumb that holds:

- **Homepage:** the chain of arguments in short form, each section with exactly one path deeper. No chapter is finished here.
- **Service page:** what is included, how it runs, what it costs, who it is for. One page per service — not four services on one.
- **Pricing page:** numbers. Even if they are ranges. A pricing page without prices costs you the enquiries of everyone who wants to know upfront whether it fits.
- **About page:** the answer to "who am I dealing with", not the company chronicle.
- **Contact page:** one path, what happens next, and how long it takes.

## The ending that costs most enquiries

"We look forward to hearing from you." Polite and consequence-free. What is missing is what happens after the click.

> Write two sentences about your project. You get an assessment within 24 hours — including a rough price range, even if we are not the right fit.

That is the same call to action, extended by what the click costs and what it returns. That extension is frequently the difference between a contact page that works and one that merely exists.
